Experiment 3 of 4 · shadows as a second view

Can a shadow tell you how far away something is?

From one photo, "near and small" looks identical to "far and large". Seismologists know this trap: a reflection time cannot separate velocity from depth. They break it with multiples, energy that travelled a different path. A cast shadow is a second viewpoint, from the light.

What you will learn

Why one photo cannot tell near-and-small from far-and-large, and how a cast shadow acts like a second view.

Key takeaway

In an idealised simulation a shadow makes depth identifiable, if it lands in frame; textured floors and a mislocated light weaken it.

See the ambiguity

Now drag it yourself. The grey disc sits on a fixed line of sight. As it moves away it grows, so the camera's view never changes, but the shadow on the floor does. Whether that helps depends on whether the shadow falls on floor the camera can see.

The Hessian spectrum, read like a geophysicist

Directions the data cannot see show up as near-zero eigenvalues of the Gauss–Newton Hessian; here Fisher information plays that role. Without a shadow one eigenvalue sits at numerical zero: the depth–size direction. With the shadow it rises to about 7, within a factor of five of the next one (about 34): observable, though still the weakest.

Where does it work?

Fisher information over 1,080 setups (nine light azimuths, five elevations, four light sizes, two floor brightnesses, three depths). Each cell shows how well depth is pinned down, as the standard deviation of log depth (0.1 is about ±10%). Stronger colour means more uncertainty; × marks cells that miss your threshold.

What it does not show

Inverse crime.Data and estimator share one forward model, so this shows local identifiability, not accuracy on real photos.
No real shadows were tested.Splat worlds store appearance as colour, so rendered photos have no separate cast shadows and any test would be circular. Stage B was skipped on purpose.
Textured floors hurt.Floor texture at a scale comparable to the shadow collapses the information (see the stress test above). Single-image shadow methods look most plausible for sunlit scenes or one dominant lamp.
Prior art and the seismic analogy

Methods that recover geometry from shadows or two-bounce light already exist, including ShadowNeuS (arXiv 2211.14086) and, with lidar, PlatoNeRF (arXiv 2312.14239); their exact capture requirements were not re-verified here. The claim in this page, one image under one natural light, is shown only in simulation.

In reflection seismology a single traveltime cannot separate velocity from depth (Bickel 1990); offset (moveout) resolves it, just as camera baseline (parallax) resolves scale. A multiple is energy on a different path that illuminates what the primary arrivals do not, and its usefulness depends on whether that path lands inside the recorded aperture. For a shadow, that condition is "the shadow falls inside the frame".
